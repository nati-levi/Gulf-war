1980s – July 1990

Seeds of Conflict

The roots of the Gulf War

The origins of the First Gulf War trace back to the devastating Iran-Iraq War (1980–1988), which left Iraq economically shattered despite claiming a nominal victory. Saddam Hussein's regime had accumulated roughly $80 billion in foreign debt, much of it owed to Kuwait and Saudi Arabia, who had bankrolled Iraq's war against Iran.

By 1990, Iraq's economy was in crisis. Oil revenues — the country's lifeblood — were suppressed by what Saddam viewed as deliberate overproduction by fellow OPEC members, particularly Kuwait. Iraq accused Kuwait of slant-drilling into the shared Rumaila oil field along their border, effectively stealing Iraqi oil.

Key Tensions

  • $80 billion war debt — Iraq demanded Kuwait and Saudi Arabia forgive wartime loans, arguing it had fought Iran on behalf of all Arab states
  • Oil overproduction — Kuwait exceeded its OPEC quota, driving down prices and costing Iraq an estimated $7 billion per year in lost revenue
  • Rumaila oil field dispute — Iraq accused Kuwait of extracting $2.4 billion worth of oil from the contested border field
  • Historical territorial claims — Iraq had long considered Kuwait a "natural part" of its Basra province, a claim dating to the Ottoman era

Raising the price of oil by one dollar a barrel would mean one billion dollars more to Iraq annually. This is a kind of war against Iraq.

— Saddam Hussein, Arab League Summit, Baghdad, May 1990
August 2, 1990

The Invasion of Kuwait

100,000 troops cross the border at dawn

At 2:00 AM local time on August 2, 1990, approximately 100,000 Iraqi troops backed by 700 tanks poured across the Kuwaiti border. The small emirate's military — just 16,000 active-duty troops — was overwhelmed within hours. By nightfall, Iraqi forces had seized Kuwait City, and the Emir had fled to Saudi Arabia.

The invasion was swift and decisive. Iraqi Republican Guard divisions spearheaded the assault, quickly neutralizing Kuwaiti air bases and military installations. Within 12 hours, Saddam Hussein effectively controlled the entire country, gaining access to 20% of the world's known oil reserves.

August – November 1990

The World Responds

Building the largest military coalition since World War II

The international community reacted with rare unanimity. Within hours of the invasion, the United Nations Security Council passed Resolution 660, condemning the invasion and demanding Iraq's immediate withdrawal. Over the following months, a coalition of 35 nations assembled what would become the largest military alliance since the Second World War.

This will not stand, this aggression against Kuwait.

— President George H.W. Bush, August 5, 1990

August 1990 – January 1991

Operation Desert Shield

The largest military buildup since World War II

On August 7, 1990, President Bush ordered the deployment of American forces to Saudi Arabia in what was codenamed Operation Desert Shield. The initial mission was purely defensive — to protect Saudi Arabia from a potential Iraqi invasion. Over the following five months, it grew into the largest military deployment since the Second World War.

The logistical feat was staggering. The United States military had to transport hundreds of thousands of troops, millions of tons of equipment, and billions of dollars' worth of supplies halfway around the world to a region with limited existing infrastructure. At its peak, transport aircraft were landing in Saudi Arabia every 10 minutes.

A line has been drawn in the sand. The United States will not allow this aggression to stand. The mission of our troops is wholly defensive. Hopefully, they will not be needed long.

— President George H.W. Bush, Address to the Nation, August 8, 1990

As diplomatic efforts continued, the military buildup accelerated. By November 1990, Bush doubled the force to ensure an offensive capability, signaling that the U.S. was prepared to use force if Iraq did not withdraw. The desert became a vast staging ground, with troops from 35 nations training alongside each other in the scorching Saudi heat.

November 29, 1990

The Ultimatum

UN Resolution 678 — A final deadline

On November 29, 1990, the UN Security Council passed the resolution that made war all but inevitable. Resolution 678 gave Iraq one final deadline — a "pause of goodwill" — to withdraw completely from Kuwait.

United Nations Security Council
Resolution 678

The Security Council,

Noting that, despite all efforts by the United Nations, Iraq refuses to comply with its obligation to implement Resolution 660 and the above-mentioned subsequent relevant resolutions...

Authorizes Member States co-operating with the Government of Kuwait to use all necessary means to uphold and implement Resolution 660 and all subsequent relevant resolutions and to restore international peace and security in the area.

Requests all States to provide appropriate support for the actions undertaken...

DEADLINE FOR COMPLIANCE:

JANUARY 15, 1991

In a last-ditch effort at diplomacy, U.S. Secretary of State James Baker met with Iraqi Foreign Minister Tariq Aziz in Geneva on January 9, 1991. The six-hour meeting ended with no progress.

I heard nothing that suggested to me any Iraqi flexibility whatsoever on complying with the United Nations Security Council resolutions.

— Secretary of State James Baker, after the Geneva meeting, January 9, 1991

The deadline passed at midnight on January 15, 1991. Iraq did not withdraw. War was now inevitable.

January 17, 1991 — 2:38 AM Baghdad Time

Operation Desert Storm

The air campaign begins

In the early morning hours of January 17, 1991, coalition aircraft and cruise missiles struck targets across Iraq and Kuwait, launching the most intensive air campaign in military history. For 38 consecutive days, coalition forces flew over 100,000 sorties, systematically dismantling Iraq's military infrastructure.

The skies over Baghdad have been illuminated... We're seeing bright flashes going off all over the sky.

— Bernard Shaw, CNN, live broadcast from Baghdad, January 17, 1991

February 24–28, 1991

100 Hours

The ground campaign

At 4:00 AM on February 24, 1991, coalition ground forces launched their assault. What followed was one of the most decisive ground campaigns in military history. In just 100 hours, coalition forces liberated Kuwait and routed the Iraqi military in a sweeping maneuver that became a textbook study in modern warfare.

The "Left Hook" — Flanking Maneuver

The strategy was brilliantly simple: while Marines and Arab forces attacked directly into Kuwait's defenses, drawing Iraqi attention, two massive armored corps swept west through the open desert, swinging north and east to encircle the Iraqi army from behind. General Schwarzkopf called it the "Hail Mary" play.

Key Engagements

  • Battle of 73 Easting (Feb 26) — A regiment of the U.S. 2nd Armored Cavalry destroyed an entire Iraqi Republican Guard brigade in a fierce sandstorm engagement. The most significant tank battle of the war.
  • Battle of Norfolk (Feb 27) — The U.S. 1st Infantry Division breached Iraqi minefields and defensive lines, destroying over 840 Iraqi armored vehicles in a single night.
  • Battle of Medina Ridge (Feb 27) — The 1st Armored Division engaged the Medina Division of the Republican Guard in the largest tank battle in U.S. Army history, destroying 186 Iraqi tanks.
  • Liberation of Kuwait City (Feb 27) — Kuwaiti and Saudi forces entered Kuwait City, finding the retreating Iraqis had set hundreds of oil wells ablaze.

February 26–27, 1991

The Highway of Death

As Iraqi forces began their retreat from Kuwait, thousands of military and civilian vehicles clogged Highway 80, the main road from Kuwait City to Basra. Coalition aircraft attacked the retreating columns, creating miles of destroyed vehicles and equipment that journalists would call the "Highway of Death."

The retreating Iraqi forces — a mixture of military vehicles, stolen civilian cars, trucks loaded with plundered goods, and tanks — stretched for miles along the highway. Coalition pilots described the scene as a "turkey shoot," though many vehicles were found to have been abandoned by their occupants before the strikes.

It was like a medieval hell — the most horrifying thing I have ever witnessed in my life. Mile after mile of burned and blasted vehicles, some still smoldering.

— Journalist reporting from Highway 80, February 28, 1991

The images from the Highway of Death became some of the most enduring — and controversial — of the entire war. The scenes of destruction played a significant role in President Bush's decision to declare a ceasefire on February 28, just 100 hours after the ground campaign began. Critics argued the war had been ended too soon; others felt the images showed a conflict that had already achieved its objectives.

February 28, 1991

Ceasefire & Aftermath

Kuwait is liberated — but at what cost?

The Ceasefire

At 8:00 AM on February 28, 1991, exactly 100 hours after the ground campaign began, President Bush declared a ceasefire. Kuwait was liberated. The Iraqi military was shattered.

On March 3, Iraqi commanders formally accepted coalition ceasefire terms at Safwan airfield in southern Iraq. The subsequent UN Resolution 687 established the formal peace terms.

Key Ceasefire Terms

  • Iraq must recognize Kuwait's sovereignty and borders
  • Destruction of all chemical, biological, and nuclear weapons programs
  • Iraq to accept liability for war damages and pay reparations
  • UN weapons inspectors granted full access to Iraqi facilities
  • Continuation of economic sanctions until terms were met
  • Establishment of a demilitarized zone along the Iraq-Kuwait border

The Oil Fires

In one of the most devastating acts of environmental warfare in history, retreating Iraqi forces set fire to over 600 Kuwaiti oil wells. The fires burned for over eight months, creating vast plumes of toxic black smoke visible from space and turning day into night across the region.

Key Figures

The leaders who shaped the conflict

George H.W. Bush
President of the United States

The 41st president assembled the international coalition and directed U.S. strategy. His careful diplomacy unified 35 nations against Iraq's aggression.

Saddam Hussein
President of Iraq

Iraq's authoritarian leader ordered the invasion of Kuwait and refused all diplomatic efforts for withdrawal, leading to devastating military defeat.

Norman Schwarzkopf
Commander, U.S. Central Command

Known as "Stormin' Norman," he designed and executed the coalition's military strategy, including the famous "Left Hook" flanking maneuver.

Colin Powell
Chairman, Joint Chiefs of Staff

Advocated the doctrine of overwhelming force. His strategic vision shaped the coalition's approach of decisive, rapid military action.

Dick Cheney
U.S. Secretary of Defense

Oversaw the military buildup and provided crucial political-military coordination between the White House and the Pentagon.

James Baker
U.S. Secretary of State

Led the diplomatic coalition-building effort and made the final attempt at a negotiated settlement in his Geneva meeting with Tariq Aziz.

Margaret Thatcher
Prime Minister of the United Kingdom

Provided early and unwavering support for military action. Famously told Bush, "This is no time to go wobbly." Succeeded by John Major during the war.

King Fahd
King of Saudi Arabia

Made the critical decision to invite Western military forces onto Saudi soil — a controversial move that had lasting geopolitical consequences.

Legacy

  • New World Order — President Bush declared the war heralded a "new world order" of international cooperation through the United Nations
  • No-Fly Zones — Coalition-enforced no-fly zones over northern and southern Iraq protected Kurdish and Shia populations, lasting until 2003
  • Sanctions Regime — Crippling economic sanctions remained on Iraq for over a decade, with devastating humanitarian consequences
  • Gulf War Syndrome — Thousands of veterans reported mysterious chronic symptoms, sparking ongoing medical research and debate
  • The CNN Effect — The first war broadcast live on television 24/7, fundamentally changing how the public experienced armed conflict
  • Precision Warfare — Showcased a new era of technology-driven warfare with GPS-guided munitions and stealth aircraft
  • Unfinished Business — Saddam Hussein remained in power, leading many to view the war's conclusion as incomplete — a factor in the 2003 Iraq War
  • Regional Realignment — The presence of Western troops in Saudi Arabia became a major grievance cited by extremist groups in the following decade

The First Gulf War lasted just 42 days of air combat and 100 hours of ground war, but its consequences reshaped the Middle East and reverberated through the decades that followed. It was a conflict that demonstrated both the overwhelming power of modern military technology and the limits of military victory in resolving deep political and regional tensions.
